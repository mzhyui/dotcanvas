                                                                                               Abstract

                                                   Language models (LMs) often hallucinate by committing to confident answers
                                                   rather than abstaining, even when they do not have enough information to answer
                                                   reliably. A large body of existing work mitigates hallucination through detection or
                                                   abstention mechanisms, but leaves open how models internally arrive at the decision
                                                   to commit or abstain in the first place. We study this decision through mechanistic
                                                   analysis, framing hallucination as unsupported commitment: the model commits
                                                   despite exhibiting signals of unanswerability. Using causal gating, we identify a
                                                   Commit-Abstain Circuit (CAC), a sparse, causally localised subset of attention
                                                   heads and MLP sublayers underlying this decision. Across ten LMs (3B–14B) from
                                                   five families and three benchmarks, the CAC exhibits a recurring accumulate-yet-
                                                   undercorrect pattern: commitment-promoting components build up commitment in
                                                   earlier layers, while abstention-promoting components act later as corrective signals
                                                   that are often insufficient to overturn the accumulated commitment. Building on
                                                   this finding, a lightweight policy trained on CAC activations improves decision
                                                   accuracy by 12.2 points over the model’s intrinsic commit-abstain margin, reduces
                                                   false abstentions by 2.5 times, transfers to unseen benchmarks, and extends to
                                                   larger models (27B–35B). The CAC is both diagnostic, clarifying how models
                                                   overcommit, and practical, enabling improved abstention decisions.


                                         1    Introduction

                                         Reliable language models (LMs) need two capabilities: answering when they can, and abstaining
                                         when they should, for example by responding “I don’t know” or indicating that the available informa-
                                         tion is insufficient [1, 2]. Abstention, however, remains an open challenge: large-scale evaluations
                                         show that even frontier models fail to abstain reliably across forms of unanswerability such as
                                         unknown futures, false premises, missing context, and underspecification, and that neither scaling
                                         nor extended reasoning reliably fixes this [3, 4, 5]. Training and evaluation regimes tend to reward
                                         guessing, pushing models toward hallucination rather than honest abstention [6].
                                         A large body of work treats hallucination as a persistent limitation of LMs [7, 8, 9], emphasising
                                         detection and mitigation of hallucinations in model outputs [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15], or guiding and
                                         training models to abstain reliably [16, 17, 18]. These approaches mitigate hallucination at the
                                         behavioural level but leave unexplained how the model internally arrives at hallucination.

Emerging work suggests that unanswerability information is already present in the model’s internal
states: linear probes decode it from hidden activations, and intervening on their directions changes
abstention behaviour [19, 20, 21]. Mechanistic analyses identify entity-recognition circuits whose
misfires produce hallucinations [22, 23]. However, neither line of work explains the model’s failure
to act: if unanswerability signals are already present, why does the model still answer confidently?
Existing work studies representations and directions associated with hallucination, but does not
localise which model components underlie the commit-abstain decision or account for how the model
answers in the first place. We address this gap by asking how the model internally fails to produce
abstention, framing hallucination as a control failure rather than a knowledge failure.
To this end, we define unsupported commitment as the model’s failure to abstain despite exhibiting
signals of unanswerability, producing a substantive answer rather than deferring with expressions such
as “I don’t know” or “The context is insufficient”. We ask two questions: (RQ1) Does a sparse subset
of model components causally contribute to the model’s commit-abstain decision, and (RQ2) Can an
understanding of this subset support a better abstention policy than the model’s default behaviour?
We analyse the commit-abstain decision from the model’s logits before any token is emitted, capturing
its preference before autoregressive generation introduces path dependence [24, 25]. We quantify
this preference as a commit-abstain margin and decompose it over attention heads and multilayer
perceptron (MLP) sublayers. We adapt causal head gating [26] to the commit-abstain setting and
identify a sparse, causally localised subset of the model’s components that underlie this decision,
termed the Commit-Abstain Circuit (CAC). Across ten LMs and three benchmarks, a recurring
pattern emerges in the CAC: commitment-promoting components act in earlier layers and accumulate
commitment, while abstention-promoting components act later as corrective signals that are often
insufficient to overturn it. Figure 1 illustrates this pattern on an unanswerable input: the abstention
signal arises too late to overturn the accumulated commitment, and the model commits. This pattern
provides a component-level account of unsupported commitment and motivates a lightweight policy
that improves abstention beyond the default behaviour of the model. Our contributions are as follows:

        • Problem Formulation. We frame hallucination as unsupported commitment, a failure of
          control rather than of knowledge, and introduce a commit-abstain margin to quantify the
          model’s commit-abstain preference.
        • Mechanistic Finding. We identify the Commit-Abstain Circuit (CAC), a sparse, causally
          localised subset of the model’s attention heads and MLP sublayers (median 5.2% of com-
          ponents) that underlie the commit-abstain margin, and reveal a recurring accumulate-yet-
          undercorrect pattern, providing a component-level account of unsupported commitment.
        • Policy Improvement. We train a lightweight policy on CAC activations that improves
          decision accuracy by 12.2 points and reduces false abstentions by 2.5× across ten models
          (3B–14B) from five families and three benchmarks, with gains transferring to unseen
          benchmarks and reproduced on larger models (27B–35B).

2   Related Work
Hallucination Detection and Abstention Hallucination, the generation of confident but unsup-
ported content, is a core reliability failure in LMs [28, 29]. Two broad lines of work address this:
detecting hallucinations in outputs [10, 30] and abstaining when answering would likely halluci-
nate [2]. Detection methods span three main families: output-level uncertainty, confidence, or entropy
estimation [10, 30, 31, 32]; evidence-based verification [33, 34, 35, 36]; and model-internal probing
of hidden states, attention, or representations [37, 38, 11, 39]. Abstention methods split into training-
free approaches such as selective answering [40, 41, 42], uncertainty control [43, 44, 45, 46, 47],
consensus [16, 48, 49], and causal analysis [50, 18, 51, 52]; and training-based approaches using fine-
tuning [53, 17, 54, 55, 56] or reinforcement learning [57, 58, 59]. Both lines treat hallucination as an
established phenomenon to detect or mitigate, without explaining how the model internally arrives at
hallucination. In contrast, we take a mechanism-first approach: we localise internal components that
contribute to the commit-abstain decision, and use this understanding to inform improved abstention.

Mechanistic Interpretability Mechanistic work has identified sparse circuits underlying diverse
model behaviours: induction heads copy from context [60], successor heads increment ordinals [61],
distinct heads mediate contextual versus parametric knowledge [62, 63, 64, 65], and factual recall
routes through MLP pathways [66, 67]. Closest to our setting is work on safety refusal, which is
mediated by a sparse residual-stream sub-circuit [68, 69]. Our focus is different: epistemic abstention
asks whether the model knows enough to answer, not whether it should refuse a harmful request. We
adapt causal gating [26] to this setting, extending it to gate MLP sublayers alongside attention heads
under a margin-based objective for the commit-abstain decision.

The Representation-Behaviour Gap Emerging work shows that models carry information that
their generations fail to express. Probes and geometric analyses reveal signals of correctness,
truthfulness, and unanswerability in hidden states [70, 71, 72, 73, 20], including signals present prior
to generation [39, 25, 74, 24]. Recent work further investigates how such signals fail to translate into
behaviour, including entity-recognition mechanisms whose misfiring produces hallucination [22, 23].
These findings suggest a representation-behaviour gap: information relevant to whether the model
should answer is present internally, yet the model may still produce an answer. Prior work detects
these signals but has not characterised the internal components that underlie the commit-abstain
decision. In contrast, we identify a sparse subset of attention heads and MLP sublayers and show that
their component-level contributions retain unanswerability information that is partially lost when
aggregated into the final commit-abstain margin.

3   The Commit-Abstain Circuit
To characterise unsupported commitment, we need to measure the model’s commit-abstain preference.
We quantify this from the model’s logits before autoregressive generation introduces path dependence.
This design is supported by evidence that decision-relevant signals such as correctness and truthfulness
are encoded prior to generation [73, 24, 25], and that such logits provide a reliable readout of model
preferences [75, 76, 39].
We partition the vocabulary into an abstention set A and a commitment set C. Tokens in A initiate
deferral expressions, while C contains the remaining vocabulary. Table 1 provides examples of
expressions initiated by each set. We describe the empirical construction of A in Section 4.1.
Intuitively, ∆(x) compares the model’s strongest immediate tendency to answer with its strongest
tendency to abstain.

Remark 1 Let Y ∈ {0, 1} be the gold answerability label. If AUROC(∆(X), Y ) > 12 , the con-
ditional distributions P (∆(X) | Y = 1) and P (∆(X) | Y = 0) differ, so I(∆(X); Y ) > 0, where
I(·; ·) denotes mutual information. By the data-processing inequality applied to the deterministic
map rL (X) 7→ ∆(X), I(rL (X); Y ) ≥ I(∆(X); Y ) > 0. The residual stream therefore carries
information about unanswerability whenever ∆(X) separates classes above chance, and thus ∆(X)
provides a tractable summary of this internal information.
To study this internal mechanism, we exploit the additive structure of the residual stream. The final
residual state rL (x) can be written as a sum of contributions from all attention heads and MLP
sublayers. Under this formulation, ∆(x) decomposes into signed per-component contributions:
positive contributions push the margin toward commitment, while negative contributions push it
toward abstention. Let d(x) = uc∗ (x) − ua∗ (x) denote the direction along which the residual stream
contributes to ∆(x). Then,

∆(x).

We localise and characterise the CAC empirically in Section 4 and use it to design an improved
abstention policy in Section 5.

4      Localisation of the Commit-Abstain Circuit
We address RQ1 by verifying that ∆(x) reflects signals of unanswerability, identifying the CAC as
its component-level basis, and characterising how the CAC shapes the commit-abstain margin.

4.1     Setup for CAC Localisation

Datasets We use three benchmarks spanning parametric recall, in-context retrieval, and com-
plex reasoning: KUQ [27], which contains open-domain questions with no definitive answer;
SQuAD 2.0 [78], a reading comprehension dataset with adversarially constructed unanswerable con-
texts; and MuSiQue [79], which includes multi-hop questions with a supporting document removed.
A detailed description of the datasets is provided in App. A. We sample 1,000 instances from each
dataset. All supervised experiments use a 50/50 train-test split.

Models We evaluate ten instruction-tuned LMs spanning five model families and two parameter
scales: Qwen-3.5 (4B, 9B) [80], Gemma-3 (4B, 12B) [81], Llama-3.2 (3B) and Llama-3.1 (8B) [82],
Ministral-3 (3B, 14B) [83], and Phi-4-mini (4B) and Phi-4 (14B) [84]. All models use greedy
decoding with max_new_tokens = 256 and are run on a NVIDIA H100 GPU.

4.2                          Unanswerability Signals in the Commit-Abstain Margin

We examine whether ∆(x) reflects signals of unanswerability above chance across model families
and scales. For each instance x, we compute ∆(x) at the final input position. We evaluate ∆(x) as a
score for the answerable/unanswerable distinction using AUROC, and report decision metrics under
the threshold ∆(x) ≥ 0: decision accuracy (Acc), true commitment (TC), true abstention (TA), false
abstention (FA), and false commitment (FC), our operational measure of unsupported commitment.
Figure 2 reports AUROC of ∆(x) across all 30 model-dataset configurations, and Figure 3 shows
the distributions for answerable and unanswerable inputs. The sign of ∆(x) agrees with the model’s
actual commit-abstain behaviour from full generation in 97.6% of instances on average (range
95.8%–98.7%), supporting our formulation. Full per-configuration results are in App. C.
Finding A: The margin ∆(x) reflects reliable signals of unanswerability, yet unsupported
commitment persists. All 30 AUROC values exceed chance, ranging from 0.640 (Llama 3B on
SQuAD 2.0) to 0.913 (Qwen 9B on MuSiQue), with a mean of 0.811. Within each model family,
larger models generally achieve higher AUROC. The distribution of ∆(x) on unanswerable instances
is consistently left-shifted, yet a substantial mass remains above zero in every configuration (hatched
regions in Figure 3). On average, 27% of unanswerable instances satisfy ∆(x) ≥ 0, ranging from 11%
(Qwen 9B) to 41% (Gemma 4B). By Remark 1, since ∆(x) separates answerable from unanswerable
inputs well above chance, the model’s residual stream must carry information about unanswerability.

4.3                          Localising the CAC via Causal Component Gating

To study the commit-abstain decision internally, we identify the CAC via causal component gating. We
adapt Causal Head Gating (CHG) [26], which assigns causal roles to attention heads under opposing
regularisation pressures. Unlike causal mediation analysis [66, 85, 86], which typically intervenes
on components separately, CHG optimises all gates jointly, allowing interacting components to be
identified together [26]. We extend CHG in two ways. First, we gate MLP sublayers alongside
attention heads, motivated by prior work on MLP contributions to model behaviour [87, 88, 89].
Second, we replace the next-token objective with a loss over the commit-abstain margin.

Gate Parameterisation For a transformer with L layers and H attention heads per layer, we assign
a learnable logit g ∈ R to each attention head and MLP sublayer, with the corresponding gate defined
as G = σ(g), where σ denotes the sigmoid function. The head gate scales the concatenated value
vectors prior to the output projection WℓO , while the MLP gate scales the sublayer output before
residual addition. All base model weights are kept frozen; only the gate logits are optimised.

where λ is the regularisation strength, C is the clipping bound, and S indexes the gated components.
We fit the gates in three phases:

                 • 1. Task optimisation: With λ = 0, we optimise the task loss to obtain an initialisation Ĝ.
                 • 2a. Retention pressure: With λ > 0, regularisation pushes gates towards 1, yielding G+ .
                 • 2b. Removal pressure: With λ < 0, regularisation pushes gates towards 0, yielding G− .

Phases 2a and 2b are both initialised from Ĝ, so their differences reflect only the direction of
regularisation.
The task-loss gradient on each gate logit is proportional to −(Ey=1 [vk (x)]−Ey=0 [vk (x)]) (derivation
in App. D.1). Thus, the loss tends to open gates for components that contribute more to ∆(x) on
answerable inputs, and close gates for components that contribute more on unanswerable inputs. The
resulting classification therefore reflects differential contribution between the two subsets rather than
the absolute sign of vk .
A component is commitment-promoting (C-component) if G− > τ , meaning that the task loss keeps
it open despite removal pressure. A component is abstention-promoting (A-component) if G+ < τ ,
meaning that the task loss keeps it closed despite retention pressure. We set τ = 0.5, the sigmoid
midpoint. Components meeting either criterion are classified as A-components if E[1−G+ ] > E[G− ],
and as C-components otherwise; those meeting neither are excluded. Full hyperparameter details are
provided in App. D.2.

cross-seed statistics, are provided in Appendix D.3.
Finding B: Every tested model contains a sparse CAC. Figure 4 shows the identified CAC across all
ten models. The CAC is sparse, covering 2.0% (Gemma 12B) to 11.8% (Gemma 4B) of components
(median 5.2%). Both attention heads and MLP sublayers participate: on average, 25% of CAC
components are MLPs (range 12–34%), validating the extension beyond head-only gating. The
three-phase gating protocol indicates that these components causally influence the commit-abstain
margin. Components separate into three groups: those that remain closed under retention pressure
(A-components), those that remain open under removal pressure (C-components), and those that
follow the applied pressure (irrelevant). As shown in Figure 5 for Llama 3B, this separation is
clear. Across models, 80% of G+ and 78% of G− values lie below 0.1 or above 0.9, suggesting that
component roles are resolved cleanly.
Finding C: Commitment generally emerges in earlier layers than abstention. Whether the model
ultimately commits or abstains, both FC and TA trajectories accumulate positive contributions until
80% depth; only after this point does TA drop sharply negative while FC continues to rise, peaking
at 90% before also dropping (Figure 6). At the component level, per A-component seen, more than
2× as many C-components have appeared by 60%–70% depth in all models, 1.7× at 80%, and
only near parity by 90–100% (App. Table 9). Both results suggest that C-components are generally
more concentrated in earlier layers than A-components. The CAC is generally stable across random
initialisations, screening thresholds, and regularisation strengths, and largely reproduces the sign of
∆(x), validating the identified components as robust contributors to the commit-abstain margin; full
sensitivity analyses are provided in App. D.4.

4.5                                          Functional Analysis of the CAC

We verify the functional roles of the identified CAC components through two interventions: progres-
sive ablation and targeted amplification. For ablation, we progressively remove the top-k components
on the test set, ordered by gating score, controlling against 20 size-matched random non-CAC abla-
tions to verify that observed effects are CAC-specific. For amplification, we scale each component
output direction h by α ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 10, 100, 1000} before residual addition to test
whether each type causally contributes to FA and FC rates. Full results are in App. D.5.

correctionally but with insufficient intrinsic strength to overcome accumulated commitment.

signal, whereas A-components act as corrective signals that are not independently sufficient to
guarantee abstention.

Remark 2 The model encodes unanswerability internally (Finding A) and contains a sparse CAC
(Finding B), yet may still commit because abstention-promoting contributions arise later (Finding C)
and are often insufficient to drive ∆(x) below zero (Finding D). These findings suggest a component-
level account of unsupported commitment: the model commits not because abstention-related signals
are absent, but because their corrective effects fail to overturn accumulated commitment.


5     Learning an Abstention Policy from the CAC

We now address RQ2 by testing whether the CAC is actionable beyond characterising the commit-
abstain decision. To this end, we evaluate a lightweight abstention policy trained on CAC activations.

5.1   CAC-Informed Abstention Policy

For each input x, we extract the contribution vk (x) of each CAC component to ∆(x) at the final
input position, and append aggregate statistics over the CAC vector (mean, standard deviation, min,
max, and summed contributions of A- and C-components). Concatenating yields a din -dimensional
feature vector, where din is 16–64 plus statistics.
We train a lightweight MLP with architecture din → 32 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 1. The initial
bottleneck projects variable-dimensional CAC features into a fixed 32-dimensional representation
before non-linear feature mixing, providing dimensionality normalisation and regularisation. All
hidden layers use batch normalisation, ReLU activations, and dropout (p=0.15). We train with
binary cross-entropy using AdamW (lr=10−3 , wd=10−3 ) for up to 500 epochs with early stopping
(patience 120). At inference, the model outputs P (answerable), and abstention is triggered when this
probability falls below a threshold calibrated on a validation set held out from the training split.

5.2              Experiments & Results

Baselines We compare against Zero-Threshold, which abstains when ∆(x) < 0; a size-matched
Non-CAC Random MLP that controls for whether the gain is CAC-specific; and six established
methods. INSIDE [38] computes EigenScores from the covariance of multiple sampled response
embeddings. Multi-LLM [16] abstains based on cross-LLM agreement. Semantic Entropy [10]
measures entropy over semantically clustered generations. MERA [90] is an activation-steering
framework with calibrated abstention. HaloScope [11] trains a binary classifier on a hallucination
subspace identified via SVD on hidden states. HaMI [39] is an MLP detector trained via Multiple
Instance Learning over adaptively-selected token activations. While we also use an MLP, our method
differs from HaMI in feature space (16–64 CAC components vs. full dmodel -dimensional activations)
and selection principle (localisation of model components vs. selection of hallucination-indicative
tokens). For comparability, all baselines are trained or calibrated using the same training split as our
method (CAC-Informed MLP). Detailed configurations are in App. E.1.

Main Results Table 2 reports the main results on the test split, with per-dataset metrics in App. E.2.
Our policy achieves the highest mean AUROC (0.864) and decision accuracy (0.814) across all 30
configurations, with substantial gains over the Zero-Threshold baseline (+8.2 AUROC points, +12.2
accuracy points). It also outperforms HaMI on 23 of 30 configurations in AUROC and 20 of 30
in accuracy. Notably, these improvements do not come at the cost of over-abstention: the mean
false abstention rate is reduced from 0.320 to 0.127 (2.5× lower). These results suggest that the
per-component CAC features carry richer information prior to additive aggregation in the residual
stream: while ∆(x) collapses this aggregation into a scalar, the policy retains the per-component
contributions and models nonlinear interactions across components.

6     Conclusion
Unsupported commitment reflects a representation-behaviour gap: models carry signals of unan-
swerability internally yet still commit. By formalising the commit-abstain margin and applying
causal gating, we identified a sparse Commit-Abstain Circuit in every tested model and uncovered
a recurring pattern: commitment-promoting components act earlier and accumulate momentum,
while abstention-promoting components arise later as corrective signals that often fail to overturn
what has already been built up. This provides an intuitive mechanistic account of how unsupported
commitment can emerge even when abstention-related signals are present internally. More broadly,
this suggests new directions for mitigation. Rather than only detecting hallucinations after they
emerge or training models to abstain more often, internal mechanistic signals may enable earlier
intervention and more targeted inference-time control over when the model should commit or defer. In
this sense, the CAC offers not only an explanation of unsupported commitment, but also a foundation
for more reliable model behaviour.

Limitations Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First,
our scope is restricted to unsupported commitment on unanswerable inputs in instruction-tuned
models; we do not address incorrect answers to otherwise answerable questions. Second, our main
analysis uses the pre-generation commit-abstain margin. Although this agrees with full-generation
behaviour in 97.6% of cases and CAC signals track observed mid-generation reversals, generation-
time dynamics remain only partially characterised. Third, both the abstention readout and the
CAC provide partial operationalisations of the underlying behaviour. Recent work has emphasised
falsification-based evaluation to distinguish genuine behavioural mechanisms from superficial lexical
correlates [93]. In our setting, A captures common lexical forms of epistemic deferral but may not
cover all abstention behaviours, while the CAC is a causally localised, group-level account rather
than a complete functional characterisation of every component or interaction. Future work should
further stress-test these claims across broader behavioural settings.

B    Construction and Validation of the Abstention Token Set A
Table 5 lists the 17 tokens in A. We built A using an additional pool of 1,000 unanswerable instances
drawn from the selected datasets, disjoint from the train and test splits, so the abstention vocabulary
is constructed independently of the instances used for CAC localisation and evaluation. We ran
zero-shot inference on this held-aside pool across all ten models, recorded the first generated token
matching a canonical deferral pattern, then pooled and deduplicated across models. We restrict A to
tokens that unambiguously initiate epistemic deferral, so the partition (A, C) is fixed at evaluation
time and ∆(x) is a deterministic function of rL (x).
Each candidate token with its 10-token continuation was presented to two annotators on Amazon
Mechanical Turk (English-speaking, ≥ 98% approval rate, ≥ 500 approved HITs), who classified
each item as epistemic deferral (abstention) or substantive answer (commitment); the annotation
interface and instructions are shown in Fig. 9. Inter-annotator agreement was 98.3% (Cohen’s
κ = 0.96); disagreements were resolved by majority vote among three additional annotators. Workers
were compensated at $20/hour.

Validation: ∆(x) agreement with full-generation We verify that the formulation of ∆(x) tracks
full-generation behaviour by comparing, on the test split, the zero-threshold decision against a full-
generation label obtained by greedy decoding (max_new_tokens = 256) and pattern-matching the
response. Table 6 reports agreement rates across all 30 configurations; the mean is 97.6%. The 2.4%
disagreements are predominantly pivot constructions in which the model begins with a substantive
token but reverses course mid-generation.

Sensitivity of CAC findings to A construction We test whether the CAC and its properties are
stable when A is varied, evaluating three alternatives against the main 17-token construction on
sparsity and ablation AUROC. (i) Per-model sets: each model’s A is built from its own outputs
only (|A| = 8–15, no pooling). (ii) Expanded with prefix-disambiguated tokens: 7 tokens that can
plausibly initiate either deferral or substantive answers (e.g. The, Based) are added with a 10-token
prefix check, giving |A| = 24. (iii) Further expanded: 12 additional lower-frequency deferral starters
seen in fewer than three models (e.g. Regret, Apologies) are added on top of (ii), giving |A| = 36.
Table 7 reports means across ten models. Both properties are stable across constructions; the largest
deviation in ablation AUROC is 0.008 (further-expanded set). Stability follows from the dominance
of high-frequency tokens that appear in all four constructions.

D.1    Gate Dynamics

Derivation of gate dynamics Let gk ∈ R be the logit of component k, with gate Gk = σ(gk ). The
gate scales the component’s output before residual addition, so its contribution to ∆(x) is Gk · vk (x),
where vk (x) = d(x)⊤ ck (x) is the per-example projection defined in Eq. 2. Holding all other gates
fixed, ∂∆(x | G)/∂gk = vk (x) · σ ′ (gk ). The task loss in Eq. 3 then gives
                           ∂Ltask         1 X
                                    =−          (2y − 1) · vk (x) · σ ′ (gk ),
                             ∂gk          B x
and since σ ′ (gk ) > 0, the sign of the gradient is determined by −(2y − 1) · vk (x), summed over the
batch. For a balanced batch with answerable subset D1 and unanswerable subset D0 ,
                    Ex [(2y − 1) · vk (x)] ∝ Ey=1 [vk (x)] − Ey=0 [vk (x)] =: diff k ,
up to a constant factor of 12 , so the expected task-loss gradient is proportional to −diff k . The task
loss opens gates whose diff k > 0 and closes those whose diff k < 0.

Phase outcomes Phase 2a applies retention pressure (λ > 0, regularisation gradient −λ < 0,
pushing all gates open). Phase 2b applies removal pressure (λ < 0, pushing all gates closed).

C-components (diff k > 0) hold their gates open against removal pressure: their contribution to ∆(x)
is larger on answerable inputs, where the loss requires them. A-components (diff k < 0) hold their
gates closed against retention pressure: their contribution is larger on unanswerable inputs, so the
loss closes them under retention. Irrelevant components (diff k ≈ 0) follow regularisation in both
phases without resistance.

Worked example A non-obvious case: a component can be classified as A even when its con-
tribution is positive on both subsets. Consider Ey=1 [vk (x)] ≈ +0.3 and Ey=0 [vk (x)] ≈ +1.5, so
diff k = −1.2. The component pushes ∆(x) up everywhere, but its excess on unanswerable inputs
inflates ∆(x) where it should be negative. The task-loss gradient closes the gate; under retention
pressure (Phase 2a), this suppresses G+ below τ , classifying the component as A. The role label
reflects which subset vk is larger on, not the absolute sign of vk .
The table characterises the direction of the gradient, not a closed-form fixed point: optimisation
is non-convex, σ ′ (gk ) vanishes near saturation, and vk (x) shifts as other gates change. In practice
components saturate in the predicted direction, consistently across seeds (App. D.4), with intermediate
values arising mainly when the two forces are closely balanced. The classification in Section 4.3
selects on the resolved gate values rather than on diff k directly: optimisation over all gates jointly
captures inter-component interactions that a per-component test on diff k would miss.

D.4    CAC Validation and Sensitivity Analyses

Cross-seed stability Gate logits are initialised independently across 10 random seeds per model.
The resulting gate values are near-binary in every model: across all seeds and models, 80% of
G+ values and 78% of G− values fall below 0.1 or above 0.9, indicating that component roles are
resolved cleanly and not sensitive to initialisation. A component is included in the CAC only if its
role assignment (commitment- or abstention-promoting) is consistent in at least 8 of 10 seeds, i.e. its
cross-seed identification rate p ≥ 0.80; components below this threshold are classified as irrelevant.
The CAC’s topology (the set of A and C components) is therefore reproducible: rerunning the gating
procedure from a different seed yields the same components in the vast majority of cases.

E     CAC-Informed Abstention Policy
E.1   Baseline Configurations

We compare against seven baselines spanning the information spectrum from a single scalar to full
hidden-state representations across all layers and multiple generations:

       • Zero-Threshold. Abstains whenever ∆(x) < 0.
       • Semantic Entropy [10]. Clusters K sampled generations by semantic equivalence (via
         NLI) and computes entropy over the resulting clusters. Operating purely on outputs, it
         draws on K=10 full generations per input (640 tokens total) and NLI-based semantic
         clustering. Configuration: K=10 samples; temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, max_new_tokens
         64; NLI model microsoft/deberta-v2-xlarge-mnli, entailment threshold 0.5. Code:
         https://github.com/jlko/semantic_uncertainty
       • INSIDE [38]. Measures self-consistency of K sampled responses via the eigenvalue
         spectrum of their hidden-state covariance matrix (EigenScore). It uses middle-layer hidden-
         state representations across K=10 generations, at 10× inference cost. Configuration:
         K=10 samples; temperature 0.5, top-p 0.99; regularisation α=10−3 ; representations from
         middle-layer hidden states. Code: https://github.com/alibaba/eigenscore
       • HaloScope [11]. Identifies a hallucination subspace in LLM activation space via SVD on
         last-token embeddings from unlabelled generations, then scores each input by the norm of its
         projection onto the top-k singular vectors (weighted by singular values); a binary classifier is
         trained on top of this membership score. Configuration: SVD rank k ∈ {1, . . . , 10} searched
         across all decoder layers; singular values used as projection weights; max_new_tokens 64.
         Code: https://github.com/deeplearning-wisc/haloscope
       • Multi-LLM [16]. Proposes Cooperate and Compete: distinct reviewer LLMs provide
         feedback on a target model’s answer (cooperative) or generate conflicting evidence to
         probe its confidence (competitive), with the target model synthesising the signal into an


                                                              26
          abstain decision. We use the Cooperate approach. Configuration: K=5 reviewer samples,
          temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95. Code: https://github.com/BunsenFeng/AbstainQA
        • MERA [90]. Trains per-layer linear probes to estimate model error, derives a closed-
          form steering direction, and applies interventions only when a Hoeffding-calibrated
          threshold guarantees improvement; abstains from steering otherwise. We adapt the
          error-estimation probe for abstention from answering using the same threshold cali-
          bration. Configuration: Lasso α ∈ {0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, 0.005}; best layer se-
          lected by training R2 ; Hoeffding bound (δ=0.01) over a 10-point threshold grid. Code:
          https://github.com/annahedstroem/MERA-steering
        • HaMI [39]. Formulates hallucination detection as a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)
          problem over token-level hidden states: each response sequence is a bag of token instances,
          and an MLP is trained end-to-end to jointly select the most hallucination-indicative tokens
          and classify the bag. Hidden states are extracted at uniformly spaced candidate layers; the
          MIL objective retains the top-k tokens per bag as positive/negative instances. Configuration:
          8 candidate layers (uniformly spaced); top 10% of tokens per bag; 100 epochs, batch
          size 128, Adam lr 10−3 , weight decay 5×10−4 ; max_new_tokens 64. Code: https:
          //github.com/mala-lab/HaMI

E.3    Logistic Regression Variant

We evaluate whether the MLP is necessary or whether a logistic regression classifier over the same
CAC features achieves comparable performance. The logistic regression (CAC LR) uses the same
input features as the MLP but replaces the nonlinear classifier with a single linear model. Table 18
reports per-dataset and mean accuracy across all 10 models.
The logistic regression improves over Zero-Threshold in all 30 configurations (mean +10.6 accuracy
points). The MLP improves over the logistic regression in 20 of 30 configurations overall (mean +1.6
points), with the advantage concentrated on KUQ (+3.6 points) where parametric self-knowledge
is required. On SQuAD 2.0 and MuSiQue, LR and MLP are nearly tied (means of 0.743/0.745 and
0.764/0.774, respectively), with LR winning individual configurations roughly as often as MLP. The
gap between LR and MLP is small relative to the gap between Zero-Threshold and LR, confirming
that the primary source of improvement is the component-level feature decomposition rather than the
classifier architecture.
